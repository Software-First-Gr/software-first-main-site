How it works

Four taps from a photo to a cartridge screenshot.

No layers, no brushes, no export settings you have to learn. The interesting decisions — which colours the hardware could show together, how to spread the error, how big a pixel should be — are the app's job, not yours.

1

Pick an image

Choose any photo from your gallery. Retro Palette asks for photo access only at the moment you pick, and it reads exactly the one image you selected.

2

Choose a palette and a pixel size

Game Boy, NES or C64 for free; PICO-8, ZX Spectrum, CGA and Virtual Boy with the Pro unlock. Pixel size runs from Chunky · 96px through Classic · 160px and Fine · 256px, or off entirely if you want full resolution.

3

Convert on device

Perceptual (redmean) colour matching, a saturation and contrast pre-boost, a per-image sub-palette chosen within the hardware's on-screen limit, ordered Bayer dithering and a despeckle pass — all computed locally, in a second or two.

4

Save to your gallery

The result goes straight into your photo library as a normal PNG. No watermark, no export queue, no “share to unlock”.

🎛️

Real hardware constraints

The NES had a 64-entry master table but could only hold about 13 background colours on screen at once. Retro Palette honours that: it picks the best sub-palette for your image, the way a cartridge artist would have. That constraint is what makes the output look period-correct instead of merely posterised.

▦

Dithering that looks like the era

Ordered 4×4 Bayer dithering is the default — the regular crosshatch texture you remember from 8- and 16-bit art. It holds up far better on photographs than plain error diffusion, which turns skin and sky into noise.

✈️

Works in aeroplane mode

There is no backend to be down and no API key to expire. The conversion engine is plain local computation, so the app works identically on a plane, in a basement, or on a phone that has never been online.

The palettes

Seven colour tables, taken seriously.

These are the commonly accepted canonical values for each machine — Pepto's measured VIC-II table for the C64, the NesDev table for the 2C02, the DMG-01's four-shade green ramp. They are pinned by golden-file tests, so the same image and the same settings always produce the same output.

Privacy

The shortest privacy story we could build.

Retro Palette collects no personal data, because it has nowhere to put it. There is no backend, no account system and no analytics SDK in the app. This is not a policy promise bolted on afterwards — it is what the app is architecturally capable of.

  • Your photos are read, converted and written back on the device.
  • Photo library access is requested only when you pick or save an image.
  • The converted file goes to your gallery and nowhere else.
  • Uninstalling the app removes everything it kept locally.
  • No account, no login, no email address, no user database.
  • No image upload, no cloud processing, no AI service.
  • No analytics SDK, no advertising SDK, no tracking identifiers.
  • No emulator, ROM or game-asset handling of any kind.

The one network call

If you buy the Pro unlock, the purchase is handled by Apple's App Store or Google Play, and the receipt is validated through RevenueCat, which tells the app whether you own it. That exchange carries an anonymous purchase identifier — not your name, not your email, not your photos. Everything else in the app is offline.

FAQ

Questions worth answering up front.

Do my photos get uploaded anywhere?

No. Palette matching, dithering, scaling and export all run on your device. There is no server to upload to — the app ships without any image-handling network code at all.

Does Retro Palette need an account?

No. There is no sign-in, no profile and no user database. Open it and convert something.

What exactly does Pro unlock?

The four Pro palettes — PICO-8, ZX Spectrum, CGA and Virtual Boy — and a higher export cap, 4096px instead of 1024px on the long side. One purchase, no subscription.

How do I restore my purchase on a new phone?

Sign in to the same App Store or Google Play account and tap Restore purchases on the Pro screen. The entitlement lives with your store account, not with a Retro Palette account — which is why there is nothing to lose access to.

Is this an emulator? Does it touch ROMs?

No, and deliberately so. Retro Palette handles no game data of any kind. A palette here is a list of colour values measured from the original display hardware, applied to photographs you already own.

Why does the same photo look different on NES versus C64?

Because the constraint is different. The NES conversion first chooses the 13 colours it is allowed to show at once, so it commits to a mood; the C64's 16 colours are all available simultaneously. Faithfully reproducing that difference is most of the app.

Which devices does it run on?

iPhone and iPad on iOS 15 or later, and Android phones and tablets on Android 10 or later.
